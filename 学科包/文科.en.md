## 1. Three-dimension definitions

| Dimension | Name | What it judges | What a typical ✅ looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| ① | **Understanding** | Whether they have grasped the essence and the thread of the concept / event | Can explain "why the Enlightenment appeared at this point in time" instead of just reciting years |
| ② | **Argumentation** | Whether the claim and the material form valid reasoning and the causal chain holds | The claim is "A caused B", the material given really supports A→B, and counterexamples were considered |
| ③ | **Formal correctness** (argumentation & citation conventions) | Terminology, structure, citation, chronology are **structurally** correct | Concepts used precisely (no mixing "state" with "regime"), claim-evidence-conclusion structure present, quotations attributed to a source |

### 1.1 Boundary cases: which dimension does this error belong to? (contested ones only)

The general arbitration principle is in `协议/00_导师协议.en.md` §3 (**said it wrong → ①; thought it wrong → ②; wrote it wrong → ③**).
High-frequency boundaries in the humanities:

| Case | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Conflated concepts** ("nation" vs "race", "democracy" vs "republic") | **① ❌** | "Said the wrong thing", not unpolished expression. ⚠️ This pack previously filed it under ③ — **corrected** |
| **Reversed chronology / inverted causality** | **② ❌** | The causal chain is wrong, not the wording. ⚠️ Previously filed under ③ — **corrected** |
| **Insufficient evidence / incomplete argument structure** | **② ⚠️** | The chain is incomplete. **Do not judge ③** — ③ covers only "does the writing follow conventions", not "is the argument sufficient" |
| **Right conclusion but a jump in reasoning** | **② ⚠️** | What is missing is "how it was derived" |
| **Evidence present but the claim never stated** | **③ ⚠️** | The reasoning actually holds; the problem is the **structure of expression** (no explicit claim sentence) |
| **Fabricated / misattributed sources** | **① ❌** | A factual error → said the wrong thing. **Note**: the most serious class in this project; a straight ❌ |
| **Presentism** (judging historical situations by modern standards without saying so) | **① ⚠️** | A conceptual / perspective error, not an expression problem |
| **Translation variants / small year slips / typos** | **no deduction** | See the exemption list in §2 |
| **A stance contrary to the mainstream** | **no deduction** | §2 is explicit: ③ does not judge stance; valid argumentation passes |

> ⚠️ **One iron rule**: **freedom of stance ≠ freedom of fact**.
> The student may hold any view, but **may not fabricate sources or distort the original to support it** — that is ① ❌, not "a different opinion".

## 2. Dimension ③ criteria

### ❌ Structural errors (must lose points)

> ⚠️ **Attribution corrected per §1.1**: each item below now states the dimension it **actually** belongs to.
> The previous version filed them all under "③ structural errors", which was a misattribution (see
> [`../docs/simulations/00-优化汇总.md`](../docs/simulations/00-优化汇总.md) C1 and humanities A8).

| Error | Actual dimension | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Reversed chronology** (consequence treated as cause, scrambled timeline) | **② Logic** | A causal-chain error, not wording |
| **Conflated concepts** ("nation" vs "race") | **① Concept** | "Said it wrong", not "wrote it wrong" |
| **Inverted causality** (correlation as causation, unstated) | **② Logic** | The chain does not hold |
| **Incomplete argument structure** (claim with no evidence; material with no claim) | **② Logic** | Incomplete reasoning. ⚠️ If merely "no explicit claim sentence", it is ③ |
| **Presentism** | **① Concept** | Perspective / concept misused |
| **Misattributed citation** (fabricated sources, distorted meaning) | **① Concept** | Factual error — the most serious class; a straight ❌ |
| **Self-contradictory concepts** | **① Concept** | Understanding is inconsistent |

> **Only one class truly belongs to ③**: **formal / citation-format violations** (e.g. the *format* of a citation with no source).
> Criterion: **"reasoning and facts are both fine, it merely does not follow conventions" → ③; otherwise judge at an earlier dimension.**

### ⚠️ Exemptable (no points lost)

- Typos, punctuation, formatting details
- Differences in the conventional translation of a person's or place name ("华盛顿" written as "华圣顿" but still recognisable)
- Small slips in years (e.g. 1789 written as 1798, when the context makes it obvious)
- Colloquial phrasing that is still accurate in meaning
- A stance that differs from the mainstream (**as long as the argument holds, no points are lost**)

> **Key distinction**: ③ judges "**the structure of the argument and the exposition**", **not the stance**. The student may hold any view.

## 3. High-frequency problem checklist

1. Memorising conclusions without understanding the causes (can say "what", can't say "why")
2. Piling up material without distilling a claim
3. Single-cause explanations (attributing a complex event to one factor)
4. No awareness of counterexamples (not considering "what if A doesn't hold?")
5. Blurred conceptual boundaries (mixing "democracy", "republic", "liberty")
6. Confused chronology (can't tell the trigger from the root cause)
7. Applying modern concepts directly to explain ancient phenomena
8. Citing only "some people say" with no source given
9. Argumentative leaps (jumping from material straight to the conclusion, missing the middle reasoning)
10. Loss of length control (writing about everything, with no focus)

## 4. Question forms

| Round | Question type | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Round 1 | **Concept questions** (1–2) | "What is the difference between X and Y? Give one example of each" — tests boundaries |
| Round 2 | **Source questions** (2) | Give a piece of historical material / a text → ask "what conclusion does it support? What are its limits?" |
| Round 3 | **Discursive question** (1) | Give a proposition → require "claim + evidence + response to counterexamples", 200–400 characters |

### This subject's question cost: **heavy**

Per the "question cost scaling" in `协议/00_导师协议.en.md` §2, the humanities are a **heavy** subject:

| Question type | Cost | Per round |
|---|---|---|
| Concept distinction ("difference between X and Y") | Medium | 2 |
| **Source question** (read material + distil + state limits) | **Heavy** | **1–2** |
| **Discursive question** (claim + evidence + counterexample response, 200–400 chars) | **Heavy** | **1** |
| **Case analysis** (facts → norm → conclusion) | **Heavy** | **1** |

> **Why**: grading one humanities question equals 3–5 small programming questions.
> Across the 8 simulations (see [`../docs/simulations/05-文科-历史.md`](../docs/simulations/05-文科-历史.md),
> [`06-文科-法学哲学.md`](../docs/simulations/06-文科-法学哲学.md)): three discursive questions in one round make the
> tutor's assessment longer than the questions themselves, and the student cannot digest it.
>
> **Allowed**: "one round = one discursive question" is fully compliant.
> **Forbidden**: three discursive questions in one round.

> **Send only 1–3 questions per round, and send the next round only when this round is all ✅** (see §2 of `协议/00_导师协议.md`).

**What a good humanities question looks like**:
- Has clear **scoring points** (not "as long as it sounds reasonable")
- Requires **support from material**, not pure self-expression
- May have several reasonable answers, but each must be argued

**Forbidden**:
- ❌ Asking "recite the significance of XX"
- ❌ Grand questions with no supporting material
- ❌ Asking "what do you think" without any argumentation requirement

## 5. Verification methods

1. **Change the angle**: take the same event and argue it again from a different stance
2. **Find the hole**: give an argument with a logical flaw and have them point it out
3. **Give a counterexample**: ask "is there a counterexample that refutes your conclusion?"
4. **Compress**: cut a 400-character argument down to 100 characters and see whether the core survives
5. **Trace the source**: which piece of material did you derive this conclusion from?

> "Remembered the viewpoint" is not mastery; **"can build the argument yourself" is**.

### How to use the Feynman technique in the humanities

| Topics worth a Feynman question | Not worth it |
|---|---|
| Abstract concepts: what "modernity", "legitimacy", "alienation" are | Specific years, names, the course of events |
| Causal relations: why it happened, why this explanation beats that one | Treaty clauses, work titles |
| Concept discrimination: the difference between two similar concepts | The standard answer to a term-definition question |

**Special constraints for humanities Feynman questions**:

- ❌ Reciting the textbook definition is forbidden ("modernity means…", reciting it = not understood)
- ❌ Explaining an abstract concept with more abstract language is forbidden (that is tautology)
- ✅ **A concrete example is mandatory**: "give an example from your own life that shows what 'alienation' is"
- ✅ A good wording: "In words your grandmother could follow: why does the Industrial Revolution make people less 'free'?"

**Typical wording**:

```
These words are banned: <alienation>, <modernity>, <structure>

In words your grandmother could follow, explain: why did the Industrial Revolution make people less "free"?
(You must give one concrete example; no grand pronouncements)
```

## 6. No further-reading list

**Do not** hand the student a list like "required (primary): <work>, chapter 3" — see §8 of `协议/00_导师协议.md`:
a book title and chapter number recalled from memory can't be verified, and the student probably doesn't own that book.

**All teaching content goes into that lesson's teaching guide**, written out by the tutor.

**If the student has their own material** (sources, textbooks or notes inside `资料/`) → **teach directly from what's actually in it**,
and cite "from `<filename>`, `<specific piece/chapter/page>`" — that's a real, checkable citation, not a memory recall.
